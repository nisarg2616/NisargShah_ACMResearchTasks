Academic papers are notoriously dense, often leading researchers to waste time deeply reading papers which are ultimately irrelevant to their work.
To solve this, S. Keshav's "How to Read a Paper" proposes a systematic three-pass method. This approach allows readers to quickly gauge a paper's relevance before committing the significant time required for a deep and critical analysis.
The first pass gives you a general idea about the paper. The second pass focuses on grasping the paper's content, but not its details. The third pass helps you understand the paper in depth.
First Pass(Bird’s-eye view): The reader scans the title, abstract, introduction, section headings, and conclusion, while ignoring the rest. This five-to-ten-minute pass reveals the paper’s category, context, correctness of assumptions, main contributions, and readability. It helps the reader decide whether to continue or stop if the work is flawed or irrelevant.
Second Pass(Grasping content): Taking up to an hour, the reader focuses on the main thrust of the work, scrutinizing key figures, diagrams, and graphs while bypassing complex mathematical proofs. Unfamiliar references should be marked for later reading. If the paper remains incomprehensible after this stage, it stems from poor writing or a lack of background knowledge.
Third Pass(Deep understanding): To fully master the paper, the reader must virtually re-implement it recreating the authors' work step by step under the same assumptions. This pass requires immense attention to detail, exposing hidden assumptions and flaws while adding new proof methods and presentation techniques to the reader's repertoire.
I think the most useful part of the paper is when Keshav talks about the third pass and the concept of virtual re-implementation, reading the paper while mentally (or practically) rebuilding the authors' work from scratch using their base assumptions. By doing this, you immediately spot subtle flaws, hidden assumptions and missing data. It teaches you how to think like a researcher.
